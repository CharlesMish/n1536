Read the study

Every sphere holds 1,536 points. Turn one until you see a gap or a close pair. Is it a chance feature of this draw, a consequence of the order in which points arrive, or part of a fixed pattern? The three inspection figures give you different ways to tell.

How to read the disk

The disk unfolds the whole surface, including the side facing away from you. A point’s angle around the center records its longitude. Its distance from the center records how far it lies from the north pole, with southern latitudes near the rim. The equal-area mapping gives equal patches of the sphere equal space on the disk. Shapes and separations can stretch, especially near the rim. The figure stays fixed when you turn the sphere.

A seed is the starting number for the pseudorandom generator. The generator picks longitude and height for each point. Reuse seed 4217 and you get this bright layer again. Choose New random and the first faint draw becomes the bright one. Its gaps move, while the rule and count stay fixed.

The square shows one sequence growing. Faint dots are the first 128, teal adds points 129–512, and the accent color adds 513–1,536. Keeping the first 512 includes every one of the first 128 at exactly the same address. Turn on Order with Sobol selected to follow one point in both drawings.

The height step is set by the final count. Rebuild the recipe for a smaller count and the old points change latitude. That makes this evenly spaced full set useful in a different way from a Sobol prefix.

What the bars measure

For each point, find its closest neighbor on the sphere and measure the straight chord joining them through space. The bars in figures 01 and 03 count those lengths from short to long. Each histogram fits its own longest chord and tallest bar. To compare the two spreads numerically, use NN CV, the standard deviation divided by the mean. It is 52.8% for the displayed Random draw and 1.7% for Fibonacci.

Turn on Spacing to locate points whose nearest chord falls below the exhibit’s reference spacing. A low NN CV tells you the lengths are similar. An integration error, the size of the largest empty patch, or a probability claim about repeated draws would each need its own measurement.
